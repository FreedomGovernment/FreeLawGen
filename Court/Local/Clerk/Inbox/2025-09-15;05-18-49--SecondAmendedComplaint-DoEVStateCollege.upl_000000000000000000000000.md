## I. Nature of the action — Rights are not merely permission from the State

1. I am Jane Doe. I bring this action principally for First Amendment retaliation under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. In 2021 I engaged in protected political speech and petition criticizing State College's vaccine requirement and certain public officials. The college punished that expression, and the resulting registration restriction continues through a distant future date. I seek damages, a determination of the college's arm-of-the-state status under Kohn v. State Bar of California, 87 F.4th 1021 (9th Cir. 2023) (en banc), and prospective relief stopping the continuing burden on my Rights. I also preserve, and do not abandon, my distinct constitutional arguments concerning arbitrary State power and the safeguards required before the State may burden fundamental Rights.

2. In 2021, while I was a State College student with complex post-traumatic stress disorder (CPTSD), I requested a religious exemption from the college's vaccine requirement. I objected to the mandate and used profanity in my protected political free speech. The college responded with a conduct charge, probation, an educational essay requirement, a nominal conduct fee, and registration restrictions that extend for many years. I assert that those restrictions effectively expelled me and conditioned my return on compelled expression acknowledging wrongdoing for protected speech.

3. I challenge both the restrictions that preceded my protest and the punishment imposed for opposing them. The direct restrictions concern religious exercise, medical choice, association, gym closures, masks, social distancing, and the vaccination requirement. I also preserve my contention concerning parental direction of an adult child's education when the parent pays tuition. The retaliation claim is independent of those direct challenges.

4. The school might not have liked my words, but I have a First Amendment Right to criticize the Government and to petition it for redress of grievances. Free speech does not become unprotected merely because the criticism reaches a State employee who is offended by it. A university code cannot place the First Amendment beneath university preference. I also contend that I have a Right to my Rights. If courts apply a strict textualist game to deny heightened protection, they must reckon with the fact that the Constitution does not expressly say I have a "Right to use profanity in an email to University personnel," nor does it expressly state the "beyond a reasonable doubt" standard. Yet those Rights indisputably exist in law.

5. The claim this Court can presently adjudicate is First Amendment retaliation for protected political speech, even though crude, together with the Kohn inquiry and available prospective relief from continuing barriers. In Troxel v. Granville, 530 U.S. 57, 65–66 (2000), the plurality recognized heightened protection against interference with fundamental Rights. Troxel is properly understood as fundamentally a First Amendment association Right seeking Fourteenth Amendment procedural protection. I invoke that reasoning because constitutional protection should follow the protected liberty. Santosky v. Kramer, 455 U.S. 745 (1982), further establishes that due process requires at least clear and convincing evidence before the State may irrevocably terminate parental Rights.

6. My objection to preponderance was therefore part of the constitutional grievance I expressed, not merely dissatisfaction with an adverse result. My speech, association, and petition Rights arise under the First Amendment and apply against the State through the Fourteenth Amendment; § 1983 supplies the enforcement vehicle I invoke. My parental-liberty argument includes my contention that parents paying for an adult child's education retain a Right to direct that education. My immunity objections are stated in Section V. These theories supplement, rather than abandon, my separately pleaded retaliation claim.

7. By "Certain Fundamental Rights," I mean actual constitutional liberties, not privileges that exist only at an official's discretion. By "Substantial Evidence Protection," I mean a factual foundation with substance rather than accusation, dislike, or an administrative label. By "heightened protection," I mean that the protected Right receives decisional weight and that the State bears the constitutionally required burden of justification.

8. I seek both procedural and substantive due process. Procedural safeguards must permit a fair determination of disputed facts; substantive protection must prevent an unjustified deprivation even when officials follow their chosen procedure. I call an adjudication that respects these protections "quasi-judicial." I characterize a proceeding that merely supplies the appearance of adjudication while disregarding them as "pseudo-judicial," or a "kangaroo court."

## II. Jurisdiction, parties, and responsibility

10. I invoke federal-question and civil-Rights jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. §§ 1331 and 1343 for the federal claims and 42 U.S.C. § 1983 as the vehicle of relief against persons responsible for constitutional deprivations under color of State law. I request supplemental jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1367 over any properly pleaded and legally available related State-law claims.

12. The college administered the vaccination requirement, exemption process, disciplinary system, and educational program challenged here. It was established under State law and receives federal financial assistance. I invoke the college's exercise of State authority for my constitutional claims and its receipt of federal assistance for my Rehabilitation Act claim.

13. John Doe was the college's Provost when he decided my disciplinary appeal. I sue him individually for his personal review and affirmance of the discipline after receiving my constitutional objections and the conduct file. My claim rests on that participation, not his supervisory title alone. I contend that holding academic office did not dispense with his obligation to protect constitutional Rights when deciding a disciplinary appeal.

14. A conduct officer initiated or administered the conduct proceedings and made the initial responsibility determination. I assert that the officer combined investigative and adjudicative roles and lacked the authority I understood the applicable Code to require. That officer is not named as a defendant in this complaint.

15. I assert that the college officials exercised authority supplied by State law and the college's Code of Student Conduct. I challenge the college's use of a statute that gives all university policies "force of law" as justification for conduct that exceeded constitutional limits. I contend that such a statute was used as arbitrary State power, not as a constitutionally sufficient justification.

16. My institutional challenge to the college's policies is distinct from the provost's personal responsibility for his appeal decision. Pursuant to Rule 17(d), I name the President of State College in official capacity, by official title rather than personal name, solely to ensure the Court's authority to grant prospective injunctive relief under Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908). The Court may order the officer's name added. I do not concede the college's asserted immunity.

## III. Factual assertions

17. I attended the college in good academic standing. I am a Christian and was a qualified student with CPTSD seeking full participation in the college's educational program.

20. I became disabled with CPTSD after years of third-party custody litigation involving my child. In an earlier state proceeding, the State awarded custody to a third party under a preponderance standard. I disputed the allegations used against me and maintain that the State used preponderance to punish protected speech and deprive me of parental Rights. The earlier proceeding is preserved only as context for my petition, disability, and constitutional argument; a direct custody challenge requires its own proper parties, forum, and procedural vehicle.

27. I objected to the vaccine and mask mandates. I contend these policies functioned as a coercive exercise of arbitrary State power, designed to force ideological compliance from political opponents without sufficient, individualized scientific justification.

32. I belonged to a group of students who advocated keeping gyms open and allowing willing participants to associate. I contend that the college's vaccine requirement, masks, social distancing, and gym closures burdened my association and prevented the proposed comparison groups from gathering.

37. On October 7, 2021, I emailed in my mandate waiver petition to the college's vaccination address. It contained profane political criticism of the mandate and of public officials.

38. My words were profane, angry political criticism connected to my objection to the vaccination mandate. I stand by my right to make that criticism and do not concede wrongdoing because of its language. I did not intend to disrupt college operations and dispute that my email caused the material disruption the college alleged.

39. The college notified me of a conduct charge alleging disruption of its programs and invited me to a hearing. I requested a different hearing officer on impartiality grounds and was instead permitted a written statement.

44. The college proceeded with the matter. Although I had submitted the permitted written response, I assert that the college treated me as having failed to appear and did not provide a meaningful adjudication of my response or constitutional objections.

45. I assert that the college did not disclose evidence establishing the actual material disruption it alleged or provide reviewable findings connecting evidence to a lawful basis for discipline. I contend that the process supplied adjudication in name only—nomine tenus—rather than a substantial factual basis for taking my Rights.

47. My registration was blocked, and I assert that the restriction extends for many years, effectively expelling me. The college also imposed probation, an educational essay requirement, and a nominal conduct fee. I regard the required essay as compelled acknowledgment of wrongdoing for protected expression.

## IV. Claims for relief

### Count I — First Amendment retaliation under § 1983

60. I incorporate paragraphs 1–59. I assert that my political criticism, religious objection, request for exemption, association advocacy, and threat of lawful suit were protected activity. Profanity alone did not eliminate that protection. Cohen v. California, 403 U.S. 15, 25–26 (1971), protects the emotive as well as cognitive force of political expression. Papish v. Board of Curators, 410 U.S. 667, 670–71 (1973) (per curiam), rejects a public university's punishment of the dissemination of ideas merely because their content offends conventions of decency. Lewis v. City of New Orleans, 415 U.S. 130, 132–34 (1974), invalidates an overbroad prohibition on vulgar or opprobrious language toward police officers. Bethel School District No. 403 v. Fraser, 478 U.S. 675 (1986), concerns a high-school assembly and expressly distinguishes children from adults making a political point.

62. I assert a direct causal connection between my protected activity and the adverse action: the charge expressly arose from my email, and the provost reviewed my appeal, narrative, and conduct file and personally maintained the sanctions despite my constitutional objection. I invoke Tinker v. Des Moines, 393 U.S. 503 (1969), and Healy v. James, 408 U.S. 169 (1972), for the limits on punishing protected speech on the basis of an unsupported forecast of disruption.

63. I seek damages from the provost for his own participation, institutional relief against the college to the extent permitted after the separate immunity and entity-liability determinations, and appropriate prospective relief from the official responsible for continuing unconstitutional consequences.

### Count II — Direct burdens on religious exercise, bodily autonomy, and association

65. I invoke First Amendment religious exercise and association and the Fourteenth Amendment's protection of liberty and bodily autonomy, including my parental-education contention in paragraph 6. My authorities include Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Department of Health, 497 U.S. 261 (1990), and the religious-exemption principles of Fulton v. City of Philadelphia and Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah, 508 U.S. 520 (1993).

67. I separately preserve the direct association challenge to gym closure and related restrictions. Healy v. James recognizes the constitutional significance of access to campus channels of student association.

### Count III — Preservation of heightened constitutional protection, strict scrutiny, and related arguments for further review

69. I incorporate paragraphs 1–59. This Count preserves arguments for further review. I do not request that this Court grant relief on the claim that the student-conduct process was constitutionally required to apply clear-and-convincing evidence. I do not abandon that claim. I assert that the challenged mandate, exemption process, discipline, and continuing restrictions deprived me of fundamental liberty through a framework that allowed accusation or official preference to substitute for constitutionally sufficient justification.

70. My objection concerns who must establish the grounds for deprivation, not only how persuasive the evidence must be. Under the Troxel and Santosky reasoning, I ask the Court to assess the severity of the deprivation, my preexisting disability and State-created vulnerability, the imbalance between me and the college, and the risk of treating protected speech as misconduct.

71. I separately contend that the combined educational debt and pressure to continue unpaid academic work created coercive dependence, and I preserve this theory expressly for further review without attributing the earlier custody proceeding to the college.

73. I describe the requested safeguard as a "factual hysteresis mechanism": a margin above an evenly balanced factual dispute before the State may override a protected Right. These analogies explain my proposed safeguard; they are not numerical definitions of judicial proof standards.

75. I plead these theories separately and, where necessary, in the alternative. I expressly preserve them for further review, including Supreme Court review. I do not abandon them.

### Count IV — Fourteenth Amendment procedural due process: failure to provide a quasi-judicial proceeding

76. I incorporate paragraphs 1–59. I assert protected interests in political speech, petition, religious exercise, and association, together with my interest in continued educational participation and the money taken through discipline.

77. I requested a quasi-judicial adjudication: adequate notice and time to obtain counsel; an authorized, impartial decision-maker; disclosure of the factual case; an opportunity to present my response, evidence, witnesses, and disability-related information; appropriate means to challenge adverse evidence; and reviewable findings connecting the evidence to a permissible ground for deprivation.

78. The college authorized a written response instead of attendance, received it, then treated me as absent. I assert that it withheld the factual case and findings needed to answer the disruption charge and treated my failure to overcome its accusation as sufficient grounds for discipline. I challenge that allocation of responsibility and the combined investigative and adjudicative roles.

81. This count independently challenges the failure to supply meaningful process even under the college's stated standard. It supplements, rather than replaces, abandons, or concedes, the heightened-protection arguments preserved in Count III.

### Count V — Disparate impact disability-related exclusion and unequal educational participation

82. I incorporate paragraphs 1–59. I preserve two disability-access challenges under Title II of the ADA and § 504: punishment of disability-related expression without accommodation, and repeated full-price tuition charges for disability-related course difficulties that exhausted resources needed to complete my degree.

83. I assert that CPTSD substantially impaired my functioning and academic performance, that I was otherwise qualified to participate, and that the college receives federal funding. I contend that the challenged practices denied meaningful educational access and imposed a disparate impact on qualified students with disabilities.

85. I distinguish disparate impact, failure to accommodate, intentional unequal treatment, and deliberate indifference. I invoke the reasonable-modification requirement of 28 C.F.R. § 35.130(b)(7)(i). My claim concerns the identified disability-related barriers, the modifications necessary for meaningful access, the college's response, and the resulting injury.

### Count VI — State constitutional and related tort remedies

88. I incorporate paragraphs 1–59. I preserve my claims of interference with expression, religion, and equal privileges under the applicable State constitution, and my contention that the college's conduct and deprivation violated applicable State procedural requirements.

90. I invoke the applicable State administrative and higher-education statutes in contesting the authority, notice, findings, service, and appeal process. I preserve those arguments without assuming that every asserted statutory defect independently establishes a federal constitutional violation.

## V. Individual accountability vs. Qualified immunity and retained Rights

91. I reject treating sovereign immunity, the provost's individual qualified immunity, and the availability of prospective relief as a single defense of "Eleventh Amendment Qualified Immunity." They concern different defendants, capacities, and remedies. My Title II ADA and § 504 claims require their own immunity and remedy analyses.

93. I request separate determinations of the college's asserted arm-of-state status, the provost's individual qualified-immunity defense, and the availability of prospective relief. I request application of Kohn v. State Bar of California to the college's asserted arm-of-state status. For qualified immunity, I contend that the right at issue is an adult public-university student's right not to be disciplined for otherwise protected political expression merely because officials disapprove of its profanity. Cohen, Papish, and Lewis, cited in paragraph 60, predate the challenged conduct and establish the relevant protection against punishment based merely on offensiveness.

94. I invoke the Ninth Amendment as interpretive support against denying an underlying liberty merely because its particular factual application is not enumerated. I do not plead it as an independent § 1983 count.

## VI. Requested relief

95. I request compensatory relief for educational, economic, psychological, and reputational injuries legally attributable to each liable defendant, including individual-capacity damages from the provost.

96. I request a declaration addressing the validity and present enforcement of the discipline and prospective removal or correction of unconstitutional disciplinary records and registration barriers by the official with authority to provide that relief. I seek prospective relief under Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), through the responsible official in an official capacity, separately from my individual-capacity damages claim.

97. I request prospective relief from continuing educational and association restrictions insofar as a concrete, redressable controversy remains. I preserve those requests, and the theories stated in Count III, for further review.

98. I request return of the challenged conduct fee if paid, or cancellation if still charged, and restitution or compensation for tuition and expenses to the extent a legal basis and defendant-specific causation are established.

100. I request other appropriate relief within the Court's authority. I demand trial by Jury on all issues triable of right by a Jury.
